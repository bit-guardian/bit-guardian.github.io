About

I'm a thesis-track M.S. student in Computer Engineering at Arizona State University (GPA 4.0), graduating in December 2026. My research is in human-centered AI: making model behavior legible to the people who rely on it and making the human side of robot learning less costly.

My thesis has two threads: using sparse autoencoders to help people predict when an LLM will fail at planning, and measuring operator workload across the ways we collect robot demonstration data. My thesis committee is Prof. Lindsay Sanneman, Prof. Ransalu Senanayake, and Prof. Nakul Gopalan.

Before grad school, I spent over two years at Park+, growing from intern to SDE 2 while running Kubernetes infrastructure for a 60+ microservice platform. At ASU, I've also taken chips from RTL to GDS by designing, verifying, and physically implementing a sparse GCN accelerator in 7nm. I'm comfortable anywhere from silicon to cluster to model.

2025 to 2026

Interpretability of LLM Planning via Sparse Autoencoders ongoing

I built a SAELens and TransformerLens pipeline that extracts SAE features from an LLM solving automated-planning problems, surfaces top-activating prompts, and auto-labels the features. A human-in-the-loop study then tests whether seeing those feature labels helps people predict when the model will fail.

// silicon

2025

Sparse GCN Inference Accelerator, RTL to GDS

I architected a three-stage datapath (transformation, aggregation, and argmax) using COO sparse accumulation, then took the 16,590-cell block through synthesis, CTS, and place-and-route in ASAP7 7nm. It closed with zero setup and hold violations across 2,196 paths, 5.26 mW of power, 88.5 ns of latency against a 100 ns budget, and clean DRC and LVS. I verified the same testbench at the RTL, post-synthesis, and post-layout stages.

  • ASAP7
